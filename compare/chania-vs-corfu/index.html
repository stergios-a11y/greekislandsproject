Our verdict

The short answer: Chania for dramatic landscapes, world-class beaches, and the most distinctive food culture in Greece — with the option to extend into the rest of Crete. Corfu for cosmopolitan atmosphere, UNESCO architectural depth, and a softer trip that needs less driving. The overall scores favor Chania (4.8 vs 4.2), but the half-point gap underrepresents how different the experiences actually are. These are arguably the two best "anchor a Greek vacation around it" destinations outside the Cyclades, and the right choice depends entirely on what you want from the trip.

Choose Chania if…

  • You want the most dramatic landscapes in Greece. Elafonisi's pink-sand beach, the Samaria Gorge, the Balos lagoon — these are bucket-list scenery, not just nice spots.
  • You value food as a primary trip experience. Crete has the most distinct regional cuisine in Greece (dakos, kalitsounia, antikristo lamb, raki rituals), and Chania's tavernas are among the best places to encounter it.
  • You're open to driving. Beach trips in Chania routinely involve 1-2 hour drives each way, and the best beaches require it.
  • You'd consider extending the trip across Crete. From Chania you can reach Rethymno, Heraklion, and even Lasithi over a 10-14 day trip, making it the base for a much larger Greek-island experience.
  • You're traveling with active people — hikers, foodies, road-trippers — and the trip's pace can absorb early mornings (Samaria Gorge starts at 6 AM for the bus) and serious driving days.

Choose Corfu if…

  • You want serious cultural depth in a compact island. Corfu's UNESCO Old Town has Venetian fortresses, French arcades, and British administrative buildings unlike anywhere else in Greece.
  • You want minimal driving. Corfu can be circled in a day; you can base in the Old Town and use buses for most of the island's highlights.
  • You're going for 5-7 days and want one place to absorb fully, rather than a road trip.
  • You value restaurants as cultural artifacts. Corfu's Venetian-British-Italian layered cuisine (pastitsada, sofrito, bourdeto) is genuinely unlike anywhere else in Greece.
  • You're flying from Western Europe — Corfu has more direct summer flights than Chania.

Beaches: Chania wins decisively, but with effort

The 5.0 vs 3.9 beach score is the biggest single gap. Chania has Elafonisi (pink sand, shallow turquoise lagoon), Balos (a remote lagoon reached by ferry or rough road), Falassarna (long sand and the best sunsets in Crete), and Seitan Limania (a tiny dramatic fjord-like cove). These compete with anything in the Cyclades for pure visual quality. The catch is access — Elafonisi is a 90-minute drive from Chania town, Balos requires either a 2-hour drive plus rough off-road or a half-day ferry from Kissamos, Falassarna is 1.5 hours west. None work as casual after-breakfast swims.

Corfu's beaches are good but rarely spectacular. Paleokastritsa has small coves between forested hills — picture-pretty but small. Glyfada and Agios Gordios on the west coast offer decent sand. The east coast (Kassiopi area) has shallower, calmer water for families. None has Elafonisi-scale drama; the strength is variety and ease of access rather than peak quality. For a family that wants different beaches each day without much effort, Corfu still works very well; for a traveler chasing iconic Greek-beach photos, Chania is the answer — if you can do the driving.

Culture: Corfu in compact form, Chania in distributed form

This is closer than the headline scores suggest (Chania 4.7, Corfu 4.8 — essentially a tie). The cultural experiences are very different in shape.

Corfu's culture is concentrated in the Old Town: the Venetian Old Fortress and New Fortress, the Liston (a French-built arcade modeled on the Rue de Rivoli in Paris), the Spianada (one of the largest squares in Greece), and dense networks of stone alleys that have barely changed since the 17th century. You can walk through 600 years of foreign rule in a single afternoon. The Achilleion Palace south of town (Empress Elisabeth of Austria's summer palace) and the Old Perithia mountain village add depth without requiring a car.

Chania's culture is layered across western Crete. The Old Venetian Harbor is genuinely spectacular — one of the most photogenic working harbors in the Mediterranean, with the lighthouse, the mosque, and the boatbuilding sheds along the inner harbor. But more of Crete's cultural substance is distributed: Aptera (a major Roman-Hellenistic archaeological site), the Arkadi Monastery (national symbol of resistance), the prehistoric site at Knossos a 2-hour drive east. A serious cultural visit to Crete from Chania involves multiple drives.

The feel of each base town

Chania town wraps around the Venetian harbor — the lighthouse on the breakwater, the Mosque of the Janissaries (now an exhibition space) sitting at the harbor's edge, the stone alleys of the old town climbing back from the water. Restaurants are concentrated in the old town and the Splantzia quarter inland — Tamam, Salis, the smaller tavernas in the back streets serving genuinely good Cretan food. The crowd skews older and more food-focused than typical Greek-island visitors. Evenings are about long dinners and harbor walks.

Corfu town is broader and more cosmopolitan. The Spianada is the social center where locals walk in the evening; the Liston has cafés that have been there since the 19th century; the small Jewish quarter (Evraiki) survives with synagogue intact. The British left a cricket club still active on the Spianada — one of the most surreal Greek-island scenes. Restaurants range from tourist-trap-on-the-old-port to genuinely refined (Avli, Venetian Well). The pace is gentler than Chania's harbor scene; the crowd more international and varied.

Logistics and cost

Both have international airports with direct European flights. Chania (CHQ) and Corfu (CFU) have similar summer connectivity to British, German, and Northern European hubs. Chania additionally has the advantage of connectivity to Athens via frequent domestic flights (40 minutes) or overnight ferry (8 hours). Corfu has overnight ferries from Italy (Brindisi, Bari, Venice) — a real advantage for travelers wanting to skip flying.

Costs are comparable but slightly tilted toward Corfu being more expensive. Mid-range hotels in high season run €110-200 a night on Corfu vs €90-170 in Chania. Restaurants run €30-50 per person on Corfu (more at top Old Town spots), €25-40 in Chania. Car rental is similar (€30-55/day). For a week, Corfu typically runs €200-400 more than Chania for comparable accommodation. The driver: Corfu's positioning as a more "cosmopolitan" destination commands a slight premium.

Note on cars: both islands score 4-5/5 on "car needed." Chania genuinely requires one for the famous beaches; Corfu you can manage without if you base in the Old Town and use buses for shorter trips, though you'd miss the western coves and inland villages.

How long should you stay?

Chania rewards 5-7 days even without extending into the rest of Crete. Two days for Chania town and the harbor. One day for Elafonisi. One day for Balos (a long day). One day for the Samaria Gorge (an even longer day — get the 6 AM bus). One day for Falassarna or the Akrotiri peninsula. A week is fully booked without rushing. If you have 10-14 days, extending east to Rethymno and Heraklion is the obvious move.

Corfu also rewards 5-7 days because of the variety even in a smaller island. Two days for the Old Town and Achilleion. Two days for north-east coast beaches (Kassiopi, Paleokastritsa, Sidari). One day for a mountain village drive or Mt. Pantokrator. One day for a southern beach or the Olive Museum. A week feels genuinely full without rushing.

The honest verdict

This is closer than the scores suggest, and it comes down to two practical questions: how much driving are you willing to do, and what cultural register do you want? Chania asks for serious driving and rewards it with landscape; Corfu asks for less and rewards with town culture. For travelers who can handle 1-2 hour beach drives and want the most visually memorable Greek trip available outside the Cyclades: Chania. For travelers who want a softer experience, value culture, and prefer to base in one place: Corfu. The biggest mistake we see: choosing based on "rugged vs sophisticated" framing alone. Both islands have both — Chania's old harbor is genuinely sophisticated, Corfu's mountain villages are genuinely rugged. The driving and the cuisine are the real differentiators. If you have 14 days and want both, the natural combination is Chania first (4-5 days), then a flight via Athens to Corfu (5 days) — but it's a real trip with real airfare, not a casual combination.

Common questions

Is Chania or Corfu better for beach lovers?

Chania, decisively — Elafonisi, Balos, and Falassarna are among the most iconic beaches in Greece. The catch is access: all three require 1-2 hour drives from Chania town. Corfu's beaches are smaller and less dramatic but easier to reach. For a traveler who wants spectacular beaches and is willing to drive, Chania is the answer. For a traveler who wants reliable beach time without a road-trip mindset, Corfu works better in practice.

Which is better for first-time visitors to Greece?

Corfu, marginally. Its UNESCO Old Town gives a strong cultural anchor that first-timers often appreciate, restaurants are more developed, and you can manage without a car. Chania is also excellent but really requires a road-trip mindset to enjoy properly — the famous beaches are all 60-90 minutes from town. For first-timers who want a softer introduction to Greek-island life with the option to extend into other islands: Corfu. For first-timers who want maximum scenic impact and don't mind driving: Chania.

Can I see both Chania and Corfu in one trip?

Yes — but it requires a flight via Athens (no direct route, and the alternative is a 12-hour ferry combination). A reasonable 12-14 day trip would be 5-6 days in Chania (with optional extension across western Crete), a connecting flight via Athens (1.5 hours total transit including the layover), and 5-6 days in Corfu. The flights are short and frequent but add €100-200 per person to the budget.

Do I need a car in Chania or Corfu?

Chania: yes, essential. The famous beaches (Elafonisi, Balos, Falassarna) require driving, public transport reaches them but inflexibly. Corfu: helpful but not essential. You can base in the Old Town, use buses for major destinations like Paleokastritsa, and rely on taxis for evening dinners. Most Corfu visitors rent a car for 2-3 days within a 5-7 day stay. The car-need scores reflect this: Chania 5/5, Corfu 4/5.

Which has better food, Chania or Corfu?

Both excellent, but very different. Chania has Cretan cuisine — perhaps the most distinctive regional food culture in Greece (dakos, kalitsounia, antikristo lamb, sfakian pie, raki rituals). Corfu has a Venetian-Italian-British layered cuisine that you won't find elsewhere in Greece (pastitsada, sofrito, bourdeto). If you have to pick: Chania for authentic peasant-tradition cooking at every price level; Corfu for refined Mediterranean fusion with historical depth.
