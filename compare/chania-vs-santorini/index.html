When to visit — overlap

Our verdict

The short answer: Santorini for an intense 3-4 night photogenic experience anchored on the caldera; Chania for a week-long real Greek destination — Crete's western region, with the most distinctive cuisine in Greece, world-class beaches, a Venetian-Ottoman harbor town, and gorge hiking that nothing else in the country offers. The overall scores are identical (both 4.8/5), but they describe completely different propositions. Santorini is curated for tourists; Chania is where people live and happen to receive visitors. These are arguably the two most-different "first-tier" Greek destinations, and the right choice depends entirely on whether you want a defined experience or a substantial place.

Choose Santorini if…

  • The iconic caldera image is the trip's purpose. Santorini's volcanic landscape is unique and the visual experience can't be substituted elsewhere.
  • You're going for 3-4 photogenic nights as a couple or special-occasion stay.
  • You want a defined Greek-island experience with limited time.
  • You can absorb premium pricing — Santorini's affordability score is 1.0/5, the floor.
  • You'd consider it the trip's anchor rather than one of several stops.

Choose Chania if…

  • You want the most distinctive food culture in Greece. Cretan cuisine (dakos, kalitsounia, antikristo lamb, sfakian pie, the raki rituals) is genuinely unlike anywhere else in the country.
  • You want the most dramatic beaches in Greece. Elafonisi (pink sand), Balos (lagoon between two peninsulas), Falassarna (long sand with sunsets), and Seitan Limania (fjord-like cove) are 60-90 minutes from Chania town.
  • You'd hike the Samaria Gorge. The 16-km descent through the White Mountains is one of the great European hiking experiences — 5-6 hours, dramatic walls, ending at the Libyan Sea.
  • You're going for 5-10 days and want to use the time. Chania anchors western Crete; the region absorbs a full week without rushing.
  • You want a Greek trip that doesn't feel "touristy" in the curated sense.

Scale and what it means here

Santorini is 76 km²; Chania (as a region of Crete) covers roughly 2,376 km² — about 31x larger. This isn't really an island-to-island comparison; it's an island-to-region one. Chania the town has 108,000 residents; Santorini has 15,500 across all settlements. The visitor experiences are completely different in their structure.

On Santorini, you're never more than 20 minutes from any other point. The trip's anchors — Fira, Oia, Akrotiri, the beaches — sit within a 15 km × 5 km area. The island reveals itself comprehensively in 3-4 days. Longer stays start to expose its small scale; you'll retrace steps.

On Chania, distances are real. From Chania town to Elafonisi (the famous pink-sand beach) is 90 minutes by car. To Balos lagoon, 2 hours via Kissamos plus a boat or rough off-road. To the Samaria Gorge starting point, 1 hour to the bus departure. To Rethymno (the next major town east), 1 hour. To Heraklion and Knossos, 2 hours. The driving is part of the trip; rental car is essential (car-need score 5/5). The reward is a region with genuine variety — coastal towns, mountain villages (Anogia, Sfakia), gorges, beaches with completely different character, and the option to extend into the rest of Crete.

The Old Town and the harbor

Chania's old town wraps around one of the most photogenic working harbors in the Mediterranean — Venetian foundations from the 14th century overlaid with Ottoman additions (the Mosque of the Janissaries, 1645) and later Greek developments. The lighthouse at the end of the breakwater, originally Venetian and rebuilt by the Egyptians in the 1830s, is the iconic image. The Splantzia quarter inland has narrow stone alleys with smaller authentic tavernas. The Topanas quarter on the harbor has the more visible restaurant scene. The Evraiki (Jewish quarter) has the restored Etz Hayyim synagogue. Walking the old town takes a full day; the depth justifies it.

This is a working Greek city — people commute to jobs, kids go to school, the markets sell to locals as much as visitors. The contrast with Santorini's curated tourist atmosphere is structural rather than incidental.

Food: Chania's strongest argument

Cretan cuisine is the most distinctive regional food culture in Greece, and Chania is one of the best places to encounter it. The basics: dakos (rusks topped with grated tomato, anari cheese, olive oil, oregano — Crete's signature appetizer). Kalitsounia (small cheese or herb pies). Antikristo lamb (slow-cooked on vertical spits over open flame). Sfakian pie (thin cheese-filled crepe drizzled with thyme honey). Apaki (smoked pork). The raki rituals — small glasses of clear local spirit served before, during, and after meals as social punctuation.

Restaurants worth knowing: Tamam (in the old Turkish baths, Levantine-meets-Cretan fusion). Salis (waterfront, refined). Akrogiali (in Splantzia, more authentic neighborhood tavern). The mountain tavernas in Sfakia (Stratigos in Hora Sfakion) for traditional antikristo. Cretan wine is also serious — the Liatiko grape produces reds nothing else in Greece does; Domaine Manousakis and several smaller producers are worth visiting.

Santorini has excellent restaurants but they're polished international/Mediterranean rather than regional. Santorini's wine (Assyrtiko, made from volcanic-soil grapes) is genuinely world-class but you can drink it anywhere in Greece. Crete's food culture only fully exists in Crete.

The beaches and the gorges

Chania's region delivers two trip elements that Santorini fundamentally can't.

The famous western beaches: Elafonisi has pink-tinted sand from crushed shell-fragments and turquoise lagoons — one of Europe's most photographed beaches. Balos sits in a lagoon between two peninsulas, reached by 4WD or by ferry from Kissamos. Falassarna has 4 km of fine sand and the best sunsets in Crete. Seitan Limania is a tiny dramatic fjord-like cove. These are 60-90 minute drives from Chania town and worth full days each.

The Samaria Gorge: 16 km of descent through the White Mountains, between cliff walls reaching 600 meters above the trail, ending at the small coastal village of Agia Roumeli on the Libyan Sea. The hike takes 5-6 hours, requires reasonable fitness, and starts with a 6 AM bus from Chania to the trailhead at Omalos (the only practical way to do it as a day trip). It's genuinely one of the great European hiking experiences. Santorini has no equivalent — the Fira-Oia caldera walk is lovely but it's 10 km on a developed path, fundamentally different in character.

Cost

The gap is significant but Chania is not "cheap" — it's mid-range by Greek standards. Mid-range hotels in high season run €350-700+ on Santorini vs €90-170 in Chania. Restaurants are €70-120 per person at Santorini sit-down places vs €25-40 in Chania (with serious places going to €45-55). Car rental is similar on both (€30-50/day). For a 5-night trip for two, Santorini runs €3,000-4,500 all-in vs Chania €1,200-1,700 — Santorini is roughly 2.5x more expensive for comparable quality. The affordability scores reflect this: Santorini 1.0/5 (the floor), Chania 3.4/5 (above average).

Logistics

Both have international airports. Santorini (JTR) has roughly 2x Chania's summer flight volume globally, with intense seasonal pricing. Chania (CHQ) has substantial European and Athens connections year-round. From Athens, Santorini is dramatically easier as a short trip; Chania is the better target if you have a week or more.

How long should you stay?

Santorini works at 3-4 nights and not longer. The trip's main beats — Oia sunset, Fira walk, Akrotiri site, one beach day, a wine tour — fill 3 full days. More days at Santorini prices is rarely justified.

Chania rewards 5-7 days minimum, longer if extending into the rest of Crete. Two days for Chania town and the old harbor. One full day for Elafonisi. One full day for Balos. One day for the Samaria Gorge (an even longer one). One day for Falassarna or the Akrotiri peninsula. A week is fully booked without rushing. If you have 10-14 days, extending east to Rethymno and Heraklion is the natural move.

The honest verdict

This isn't really a head-to-head comparison; the islands solve completely different problems. For a defined 3-4 night experience with iconic visuals and romantic atmosphere at premium prices: Santorini. For a substantive 5-7+ day Greek trip with food, beaches, hiking, history, and the option to extend into Crete: Chania. Most travelers who frame this as "which is better" miss the underlying question, which is what kind of Greek trip you actually want. If you're young, traveling as a couple, going for the first time with limited time: Santorini delivers what its image promises. If you're older, traveling with family, going for a longer break, or returning to Greece after previous visits: Chania has substantially more to offer at a fraction of the cost. The two combined work very well — 3 nights Santorini + 4-5 nights Chania, with a connecting flight via Athens (1.5 hours total transit) — and the contrast itself becomes the trip's structure. Many readers who do this combination tell us Chania surprised them more, even when Santorini was the original draw.

Common questions

Should I visit Chania or Santorini for a first trip to Greece?

Depends on time and trip purpose. For a focused 3-4 night first trip aimed at the iconic Greek-island experience (photos, romance, caldera): Santorini. For a 5-7+ day first trip that introduces you to a substantial Greek destination (food, beaches, hiking, history): Chania. Most first-time visitors with limited time and a 'must-see Greece' goal choose Santorini and aren't wrong — it delivers exactly what its image promises. Most travelers with more time who want depth choose Chania and don't regret it. Combining both works very well if you have 8+ days.

How much cheaper is Chania than Santorini?

Substantially — roughly 2-3x for comparable quality. Mid-range hotels in high season run €350-700+ on Santorini vs €90-170 in Chania. Restaurants are €70-120 per person at Santorini sit-down places vs €25-40 in Chania. For a 5-night trip for two, Santorini runs €3,000-4,500 all-in vs Chania €1,200-1,700. A full week in Chania can cost what a long weekend in Santorini costs. Chania isn't 'cheap' by Greek standards — it's mid-range — but the gap with Santorini is real.

Is Chania really part of Crete? Can I see other parts of Crete from there?

Yes — Chania is the western region of Crete, the largest Greek island. From Chania, Rethymno is 1 hour east, Heraklion (with Knossos) is 2 hours east, and Agios Nikolaos and the Lasithi plateau are 3-4 hours east. A 10-14 day Crete trip can comfortably base in Chania for 4-5 days, then move east to see Rethymno, Heraklion, and beyond. For travelers wanting to discover Greece's largest island broadly, Chania is one of the better starting points (Rethymno or Heraklion also work).

Can I combine Chania and Santorini in one trip?

Yes — by ferry (4-5 hours) or by flight via Athens (1.5 hours total transit). A common 8-10 day arrangement is 3 nights Santorini + 4-5 nights Chania, with a connecting flight via Athens. Both islands have international airports if you'd prefer to fly into one and out of the other. The contrast between the two — Santorini's iconic visual concentration followed by Chania's substantive depth — itself becomes the trip's strongest feature.

What is the Samaria Gorge and is it really that hard?

The Samaria Gorge is a 16 km descent through Crete's White Mountains, between cliff walls reaching 600 meters above the trail, ending at the Libyan Sea. The hike takes 5-6 hours and requires the 6 AM bus from Chania to the trailhead at Omalos. It's not technically difficult — the trail is clearly marked and well-maintained — but it is genuinely long, with descents that strain the knees on the second half. Reasonable fitness, good shoes, and 2-3 liters of water are essential. For most adults in decent shape, it's doable and one of the great hiking experiences in Europe. For older travelers or anyone with knee issues, consider the Imbros Gorge instead (3 km, 2-3 hours, much easier).
