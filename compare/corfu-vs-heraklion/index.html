Our verdict

The short answer: Green Ionian elegance against Crete's busiest region — and honestly, two different countries' worth of holiday. Corfu (4.2) is Venetian: an elegant UNESCO old town, olive-covered hills falling into blue coves, and the only part of Greece the Ottomans never took. Heraklion (4.2) is the gateway to Minoan civilisation — Knossos sits 5km from the city centre and the archaeological museum holds the world's finest collection of Minoan art — plus the wine villages around Peza and a nightlife scene (4.7) that runs all year because 173,000 people actually live there. Identical totals, completely different weeks.

Choose Corfu if…

  • You want the beaches. 3.9 against 3.5, and the west coast delivers: Glyfada's kilometre of golden sand, the six emerald coves of Paleokastritsa, and Mirtiotissa, which Lawrence Durrell called the most beautiful in the world.
  • You want a town to walk rather than a city to navigate. The UNESCO old town's kantounia, the Museum of Asian Art, and aperitivo under the arcades of the Liston, built to mimic the Rue de Rivoli.
  • You want a set-piece Easter. The Botides pot-smashing on Holy Saturday at 11am is the one Greek Easter ritual people plan a trip around, and April rates "great" here.
  • You're travelling with children. Acharavi and Roda on the north coast are long, shallow and calm, and the island is big enough that the olive-grove interior stays quiet through August.
  • You want something that isn't quite Greece. Venetian and British colonial layers, pastitsada and sofrito at Το Δημαρχείο on the Town Hall square, and the Achilleion palace built for Empress Elisabeth in 1890.

Choose Heraklion if…

  • Archaeology is the trip, not a stop on it. Knossos is 5km from the centre, and the museum holds the Phaistos Disc, the Snake Goddesses and the bull-leaping fresco. Culture scores 5.0 — the top mark we give.
  • You'll rent a car. Car need is 5.0: the Lasithi plateau at 840m, the Dikteon Cave, Phaistos over the Messara plain and Matala's cave cliffs are drives, not bus rides.
  • You want a city that isn't performing for you. Korai and Chandakos fill with locals year-round, 1866 Market Street does the best street food in Crete, and Peskesi cooks Cretan heritage food in a restored mansion.
  • Food and wine are the point. Dakos, graviera, bougatsa and tsikoudia, plus the Kotsifali and Mandilari reds tasted at the wineries around Peza, twenty-five minutes from the city.
  • You're coming outside summer. March, May, October and November all rate "great", the museum is air-conditioned, and Knossos is better in cool light than at 35°C.

The car question splits the two weeks

Both get five days in our itineraries, and both spend them driving — but the distances aren't comparable. Corfu is 593 km²: the town to Paleokastritsa is under an hour, the southern tip and back fills a day, and the west-coast beaches string along one drivable coast. You can run a good Corfu week from two bases — Corfu Town, then Paleokastritsa or Sidari — and rarely drive more than an hour at a stretch. Heraklion's region is 2,641 km² and takes the maximum 5.0 for car need for a reason: the Lasithi plateau day is 65km and eighty minutes of driving before you've stopped anywhere, and the run south to Phaistos and Matala is 80km over the mountains, which is why the plan has you sleep in Matala rather than come back.

If nobody in your group wants to drive, this is decisive. Corfu without a car is still a holiday: the old town, the Liston, boats out of Paleokastritsa, a resort beach on the north coast. Heraklion without a car is Knossos, the archaeological museum, Market Street and Agia Pelagia for a sunset swim — genuinely three excellent days — and then the plateau, the wine villages, Phaistos, Gortyna and the whole south coast are out of reach. The archaeology alone justifies the trip; the other half of the week needs wheels, and budgeting for them changes the cost comparison more than the 3.5-against-3.2 affordability scores suggest.

Two flights, no ferry: combining them, and which month

There is no sea route between these two. Corfu has no Piraeus ferry at all — you arrive by direct flight, by the 1.5-hour shuttle from Igoumenitsa, or from Italy via Bari, Ancona or Venice — while Heraklion is served by the daily overnight from Piraeus, 8–9 hours, €40–70 deck and €70–120 for a cabin. Combining them means two flights with Athens in the middle, so if you're doing both, give each the five days it's rated for and treat them as separate holidays rather than legs of a tour. Under ten days, choose one. The natural second island for Corfu is elsewhere in the Ionian; for Heraklion it's the rest of Crete, by road.

September is the answer on both, for different reasons: Corfu has the Ionian at its best, with charters thinning, prices softening and the green interior rebounding from the heat, while Heraklion's note for the month is simply "Knossos breathes". July and August are where they part company. Corfu rates both "great" because the island is large enough to escape into — Kavos gets raucous, the north and the olive-grove interior stay calm — while Heraklion drops to "ok" for both, because Knossos under a 35°C midday sun is punishment and the air-conditioned museum is the honest fallback. For a first Greek summer holiday in August, Corfu. For anything from October to April, Heraklion, comfortably.

The honest tiebreaker is atmosphere: Corfu feels like a holiday, Heraklion feels like Greece with a holiday attached. Families and first-timers: Corfu. Archaeology, food and city energy: Heraklion — and rent the car (5.0), because the region only opens up if you drive. Verdict: Corfu for charm, Heraklion for depth.

Common questions

Can I visit both Corfu and Heraklion in one trip?

Only by flying, with Athens in the middle. There is no ferry between them — Corfu has no Piraeus connection at all, reaching the mainland via Igoumenitsa in 1.5 hours, while Heraklion runs the daily 8–9 hour overnight from Piraeus. Both are rated at five days, so combining them properly needs about ten days. Under that, pick one.

Which is cheaper, Corfu or Heraklion?

Heraklion, marginally — 3.5 for affordability against Corfu's 3.2 — but the car changes the maths. Heraklion scores the maximum 5.0 for car need, so a rental for most of the week is not optional if you want Lasithi, Phaistos or Matala. Corfu at 4.0 is more forgiving: two bases and shorter drives, and a car-free week is still a real holiday.

Which is better for families?

Corfu. Beaches score 3.9 against 3.5, and the north coast gives you Acharavi and Roda — long, shallow and calm — plus Glyfada's gradual sandy entry on the west. Drives are shorter, and the island is big enough that the olive-grove interior stays quiet in August. Heraklion's family answer is Malia and Agia Pelagia, but the week is built around archaeology and driving.

Which is better in October?

Heraklion, clearly. October rates "great" there for last swims and museums, November stays mild, and December still has the city alive — Heraklion is a working capital of 173,000 people, not a season. Corfu's October is good too, with last swims and the old town, but by December it's mild and wet, and the resorts have shut.

Do I need to rent a car on each island?

On Heraklion, yes — car need is 5.0, and the Lasithi plateau day alone is 65km with eighty minutes of driving. Without one you get Knossos, the archaeological museum, 1866 Market Street and a swim at Agia Pelagia, and little else. On Corfu (4.0) a car is recommended for the west-coast beaches, but the old town, the Liston and Paleokastritsa boat trips work without one.
