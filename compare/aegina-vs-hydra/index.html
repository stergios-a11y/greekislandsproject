Our verdict

The short answer: Aegina if you want a real, working Greek island within ninety minutes of Athens — population 13,000, a proper market town, the famous PDO pistachio harvest, and a serious archaeological site in the Temple of Aphaea. Hydra if you want atmosphere over substance — the car-free harbour, the artist legacy, and a quality of quiet that almost no other accessible island offers. The overall scores tell the story: Hydra at 4.0 reflects its unique atmospheric value, Aegina at 3.3 reflects a more mundane but more livable experience. Both are good choices, but for entirely different visitors.

Choose Aegina if…

  • You want the easiest possible Greek-island trip from Athens. Aegina is 40 minutes by fast ferry, 75 by conventional — and the conventional ferry is meaningfully cheaper.
  • You value an authentic working-island feel. Aegina has fishermen, farmers, a real town with hardware shops and bakeries, and a population that doesn't depend solely on tourism.
  • You're interested in archaeology. The Temple of Aphaea (a 5th-century BC Doric temple) forms the "sacred triangle" with the Parthenon and the Temple of Poseidon at Sounio — geometrically related, visible from each other on clear days.
  • You're traveling with extended family or in a group that values comfort, predictability, and good food. Aegina's pistachio harvest in September is genuinely one of the best food experiences in the Saronic.
  • You'd consider a day trip rather than an overnight. Aegina is the only Saronic island that genuinely works as a day trip from Athens — Hydra rewards 2-3 nights at minimum.

Choose Hydra if…

  • You want a singular, atmospheric experience. The harbour, the bell tower, the cobbled streets, the donkeys instead of cars — nothing else like it in Greece.
  • You're going for 2-3 nights, not a day trip. Hydra rewards short, immersive stays where you absorb the place rather than visiting it.
  • You appreciate the artistic and intellectual layer. Leonard Cohen lived here for years; the Hydra School of Fine Arts is active each summer; the island has been a refuge for painters and writers for sixty years.
  • You're traveling as a couple or with friends who can handle the no-cars-no-bikes constraint as a feature, not a limitation.
  • You're combining it with Athens for a city-and-island week — Hydra is the better island side of that combination because the contrast with the city is sharper.

The car-free question is the real one

This decision often gets framed around beaches, food, or distance from Athens, but it's actually about cars. Aegina has full road access — buses, taxis, scooters, rental cars — and visitors regularly drive across the island to the Temple of Aphaea, to the eastern beaches at Agia Marina, or to the pistachio farms. Hydra has banned all cars, motorcycles, and bicycles (the last by community choice, not law). The only transport is donkey, foot, or sea-taxi.

This single difference shapes everything else. On Aegina, you can plan a beach day at one place, lunch at another, and dinner at a third — fifteen minutes apart. On Hydra, you walk everywhere within town, or you wait for a sea-taxi (€40-80 round trip) to a remote beach. The result is that Aegina days feel like normal travel days and Hydra days feel like meditation. For 2-3 nights this is magical; for a week it becomes constraining. For a day trip, Hydra is technically possible but you'd miss the point — the magic of Hydra is night-time, when the harbour empties of day-trippers and the silence becomes total.

Beaches: both modest, Aegina slightly better

Neither island is a beach destination. Aegina scores 2.5, Hydra 2.2 — both below the Cycladic average. The difference in practice is bigger than the 0.3-point gap suggests, because of accessibility.

Aegina's beaches are easy to reach. Agia Marina on the east coast has a long sand strip and shallow water, working well for families. Marathonas (just south of town) is closer and has small organized beaches. Souvala on the north is quieter. Klima and Vagia are smaller coves further around the coast — drivable in 20 minutes. None are spectacular, but they're plentiful and accessible.

Hydra has perhaps six small swimmable spots, all reached either by walking (Vlychos at 25 minutes, Mandraki at 20) or by sea-taxi (Bisti and Agios Nikolaos in the southwest). The water is clean, the swimming is good, but a beach day in the Mykonos sense is not possible. Most Hydra visitors do one beach session per day at most, treating swimming as a side activity rather than the trip's center.

Food, atmosphere, and what each town actually feels like

Aegina town wraps around a working harbor where the fish-market boats unload each morning. The pistachio shops are concentrated here — Skala-style spaces selling the PDO pistachios that have made Aegina famous in Greek food culture. Restaurants are a mix: tourist-tavernas on the waterfront, better serious places one street back (Skotadis, Tsias). The food culture is robust — Aegina is one of those islands where you can eat genuinely well without paying Mykonos prices.

Hydra's harbour is one of the great urban harbours of Greece — a natural amphitheatre of stone mansions rising up the hillside, painted in restrained whites and ochres, with a hidden cannon-emplacement above. Cohen's house is on a small alley above the port. The Lazaros Koundouriotis mansion (now a museum) and the fine arts school give the town an intellectual layer that other Saronic islands lack. Dining options are fewer than Aegina but generally pricier and more polished — Pirate, Sunset, and the upscale Techne stand out. The crowd skews Athenian-cultured, art-world, and well-traveled European.

Logistics and cost

Both are reached by ferry from Piraeus Gate E8 — Aegina in 40 minutes by Flying Dolphin or 75 by conventional ferry (€10-18 each way); Hydra in 1.5 hours by Flying Dolphin or Cat (€30-45 each way). The cost difference adds up over multiple trips. Neither island has an airport — the trip starts in Athens.

Aegina is meaningfully cheaper. Mid-range hotels in high season run €70-140 a night on Aegina vs €180-450 on Hydra. Restaurants run €25-40 per person on Aegina vs €40-70 on Hydra. For a long-weekend two-night trip for two, expect Aegina to cost around €400-600 all-in, Hydra €700-1200. The premium reflects Hydra's reputation more than its actual additional value — both islands deliver good food and a comfortable stay, but Hydra prices like a destination island while Aegina prices like a weekend getaway.

How long should you stay?

Aegina works as a day trip (8-hour return) or a 2-3 night weekend. The day trip is enough for the harbour, lunch, and either the Temple of Aphaea or a beach — not both. A weekend lets you do all three plus see Perdika (the smaller fishing village south of town), the Hellanion Pantheon site at Mt. Hellanion, and have one slower morning.

Hydra works at 2-3 nights, ideally not as a day trip. The town can be walked through in an hour, but the magic happens at night when the harbour empties of visitors. A morning swim, an afternoon walk to Vlychos, dinner in town, and bed — repeat for three days, and you'll absorb the place. Going for 4+ nights begins to expose the island's limited variety; the dimensions don't refresh after about three days.

The honest verdict

This is more decisive than most Saronic comparisons. For a day trip from Athens: Aegina. For a 2-3 night escape: Hydra, almost always. For a longer week-long trip: neither, really — the Saronic islands are short-stay destinations. The most common mistake is treating Hydra as a beach-destination alternative to a Cycladic island — it isn't, and visitors who go expecting Mylopotas or Sarakiniko leave disappointed. Treat Aegina as Athens's weekend backyard (food, family, archaeology) and Hydra as a single concentrated atmospheric experience (silence, harbour, art). Both are great in their own register; the failure mode is choosing the wrong register. If you have time for both in a longer Greece trip, the cleanest combination is: weekend on Aegina early in the trip, then Hydra at the end as a reset after busier destinations.

Common questions

Is Aegina or Hydra better for a day trip from Athens?

Aegina, decisively. The 40-minute ferry by Flying Dolphin or 75-minute conventional ferry from Piraeus gives you a real day on the island with time for the harbor, lunch, and either the Temple of Aphaea or a beach. Hydra technically works as a day trip but you'd miss the magic — Hydra's appeal is the evening silence after the day-trippers have left, which by definition you can't experience on a day trip. For overnight stays, the calculation reverses entirely.

Can you visit both Aegina and Hydra in one trip?

Yes — and many travelers do. The fast ferry from Piraeus runs the Saronic route and stops at both islands (Aegina first, then Poros, then Hydra, then Spetses). A common 4-5 night Saronic itinerary is 1 night Aegina, 2-3 nights Hydra. Going in that order gives you the casual working-island first, then the atmospheric one as the trip's centerpiece.

Are there cars on Aegina?

Yes — full road infrastructure. Aegina has buses, taxis, rental cars, and scooters. Most visitors rent a scooter or a small car to see the Temple of Aphaea, drive to Agia Marina beach, or visit Perdika village in the south. Hydra is the opposite: cars, motorcycles, and even bicycles are banned. The contrast is the single biggest practical difference between the two islands.

How expensive is Hydra compared to Aegina?

Hydra runs about 2-3x the cost of Aegina across hotels and restaurants. Mid-range hotels in high season are €180-450 a night on Hydra vs €70-140 on Aegina. Restaurants are €40-70 per person on Hydra vs €25-40 on Aegina. For a two-night trip for two, expect Hydra to cost €700-1200 all-in and Aegina €400-600. The premium reflects Hydra's reputation more than dramatic differences in actual quality.

Which is better for families with kids?

Aegina, clearly. The road access means easy beach hops, restaurants are family-friendly across price points, and there's enough variety to keep kids occupied for a weekend (Temple of Aphaea is genuinely interesting, Agia Marina has a sandy beach with shallow water). Hydra's lack of cars and limited beach access make it harder with young kids — beach trips require sea-taxis (expensive) or long walks. For older kids or teenagers who can appreciate atmosphere, Hydra works fine.
