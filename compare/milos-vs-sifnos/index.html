Milos vs Sifnos

Our verdict

The short answer: Milos, and by more than these islands' reputations suggest — 4.7 to 3.9, one of the wider gaps between two Cyclades we rate. Milos scores a perfect 5.0 for beaches, the only island on this site that does, because its volcanic coast produced Sarakiniko, Kleftiko and around seventy beaches of genuinely different types. Sifnos answers with culture 4.0 to 3.5, the best food in the Cyclades, and a price advantage that is growing: 3.5 against Milos's 2.8. Milos is the island you look at. Sifnos is the island you eat.

Choose Milos if…

  • Beaches and landscape are the point. A 5.0 beach score, unique on this site: Sarakiniko's white volcanic moonscape, the ochre and rust cliffs of Paliochori, Firiplaka, and Tsigrado down a rope and ladder.
  • You want Kleftiko — a collapsed formation of white sea caves and arches, reachable only by boat, and the best day on the water in the Cyclades.
  • You want variety. Seventy-odd beaches on 151 km², and because the rock changes colour every few kilometres, they genuinely do not repeat.
  • You want the painted syrmata at Klima — fishermen's boathouses with doors in primary colours, built into the rock at the waterline.
  • You will get in a kayak or a boat. Much of the best of Milos is only visible from the sea, and that is not a marketing line here.

Choose Sifnos if…

  • Dinner matters as much as the beach. Sifnos has the strongest food culture in the Cyclades, and it is not an accident: Nikolaos Tselementes, who wrote the cookbook that standardised modern Greek cooking, was born here.
  • You want to walk. Around 100 km of restored and signposted footpaths connect the villages — one of the best trail networks in the islands.
  • You want villages over coastline. Apollonia, Artemonas and above all Kastro, a medieval village on a cliff with the Seven Martyrs chapel on a rock below it.
  • You want the cheaper island — 3.5 against 2.8. Milos has become expensive quickly; Sifnos has not, yet.
  • You want pottery. Sifnos has thrown clay for four thousand years and the workshops are still working, not staged.

Milos is a geology lesson you can swim in

The 5.0 is the only perfect beach score on this site and it comes from the rock rather than the sand. Milos is a volcano that has been quarried since antiquity — obsidian first, then bentonite and perlite, which are still mined today — and the result is a coastline that changes colour and texture every few kilometres. Sarakiniko is white pumice scoured into something that reads as lunar. Paliochori is red, ochre and rust, with hot springs seeping up through the sand. Firiplaka is striped. Tsigrado is a slot in the cliff you descend by rope and wooden ladder. Kleftiko, on the southwest coast and reachable only by boat, is a collapsed white massif full of arches and caves.

Its cultural score of 3.5 is respectable and specific: the early Christian catacombs are among the most important outside Rome, there is a Hellenistic theatre above Klima, and the Venus de Milo was found here in 1820 — though you will have to go to the Louvre to see her, which locals will tell you about.

Sifnos is the better island to spend an evening on

4.0 for culture against 3.5, and it is earned in villages rather than sites. Kastro is the one that stops people: a medieval settlement wrapped around a cliff top, with Venetian coats of arms still set in the walls and the chapel of the Seven Martyrs on a rock in the sea below it. Apollonia is the working chora, Artemonas the handsome one, Chrysopigi's monastery sits on its own spit of rock, and Vathy and Faros are the quiet bays.

Between them runs the thing that most surprises first-time visitors: a marked, restored network of around 100 km of old stone paths. You can spend a week on Sifnos without a car and walk somewhere different every day, which on Milos would be impossible.

And the food. Sifnos treats cooking as a local identity, with clay-pot dishes — mastelo, lamb slow-cooked in a ceramic vessel, and chickpea revithada baked overnight — served in restaurants from Apollonia to Vathy. The Tselementes connection is real: his 1910 cookbook is why a great deal of what people now consider standard Greek cooking looks the way it does.

Where Sifnos loses, honestly

The beaches. 3.8 against 5.0 is not a close call, and Sifnos's coast is pleasant rather than remarkable — Platys Gialos is a long organised strand, Vathy is sheltered and shallow, Chrysopigi and Apokofto are the pretty pair, Cheronissos is the remote one. If you have come to the Cyclades primarily to swim somewhere extraordinary, Sifnos will feel like a downgrade and Milos will not.

Practicalities

Milos has an airport with Athens flights, which is why it scores 3.2 for access despite being further out; Sifnos has none and scores 3.5 on ferry frequency alone, being on the busy western Cyclades line. Both need a car for four days, both are reachable from Piraeus in three to five hours depending on the boat.

They also combine well — they are close, on connecting routes, and a week split between them covers both halves of the argument.

The honest verdict

Milos is the better island by our scoring and it is not marginal: 4.7 is among the highest totals here, driven by a coastline nothing in the Cyclades matches. Choose Sifnos deliberately, not as a compromise — for the food, the walking, the villages and the fact that it costs meaningfully less and has not yet been discovered the way Milos has in the last few years. If you only want one, and swimming is why you are going, it is Milos. If you would rather walk to dinner than take a boat to a cave, it is Sifnos.
