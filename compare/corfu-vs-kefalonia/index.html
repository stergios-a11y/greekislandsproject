Our verdict

The short answer: Corfu for cultural depth, food, and an easier first-time-in-Greece trip. Kefalonia for natural drama, better beaches, and road-trip travelers. The overall scores are nearly identical (Corfu 4.2, Kefalonia 4.1), but the islands deliver fundamentally different versions of "the lush green Greek island" and which is right for you depends on what you most want from the trip.

Choose Corfu if…

  • You want serious cultural depth. The UNESCO-listed Old Town has Venetian fortresses, French arcades, and British administrative buildings unlike anywhere else in Greece.
  • This is your first trip to Greece and you want a well-rounded introduction — beaches, history, food, hill villages, even a literary heritage (Lawrence Durrell, Edward Lear, Gerald Durrell).
  • You're flying in from Western Europe — Corfu has more direct flights and a denser summer schedule than Kefalonia.
  • You value restaurants and food culture. Corfu has Venetian, British, and Italian influences producing dishes (pastitsada, sofrito, bourdeto) you won't find elsewhere in Greece.
  • You'd consider a day trip to Paxos or even Albania — Corfu is the regional ferry hub.

Choose Kefalonia if…

  • You want one of the most dramatic coastlines in Greece. Myrtos Beach with its white cliffs is among the most photographed beaches in the country, and the score reflects it (beach 4.7 vs Corfu's 3.9).
  • You want a road trip — Kefalonia is huge (773 km², the largest Ionian island) with pine-forested mountains and a serious driving culture.
  • You care about wine. The indigenous Robola grape produces serious whites that don't exist elsewhere in Greece.
  • You're traveling as a couple or with active friends. Hiking Mt. Ainos, the Drogarati and Melissani caves, and exploring tiny villages reward this kind of trip.
  • You want quieter evenings. Kefalonia is more rural and less developed than Corfu — outside Argostoli, the rhythm is tavernas and early nights.

Beaches: Kefalonia wins, and it's significant

The 4.7 vs 3.9 beach score is the most consequential number in this comparison. Kefalonia's coastline is shaped by tall limestone cliffs falling into turquoise water — Myrtos Beach is the iconic example (a long pebble crescent backed by 200-meter cliffs), but the entire west coast follows this pattern. Petani is a quieter alternative with similar drama. Antisamos has clear water and the Captain Corelli's Mandolin film association. Fteri Beach is reached only by boat or a 25-minute hike. The variety and visual quality of Kefalonia's beaches genuinely competes with the best of the Cyclades.

Corfu's beaches are good but rarely spectacular. Paleokastritsa has small coves between forested hills — picture-pretty but small. Glyfada and Agios Gordios on the west coast offer decent sand. The east coast (Kassiopi area) has shallower, calmer water. None of Corfu's beaches has Myrtos-scale drama; the island's strength is variety rather than peak quality. For a family that wants different beaches each day without much effort, Corfu still works well; for a traveler chasing iconic Greek-beach photos, Kefalonia is the answer.

The feel of each island

Corfu is the most cosmopolitan Greek island outside Athens and Mykonos. The Old Town is a UNESCO World Heritage Site: the Venetian Old Fortress and New Fortress, the Liston (a French-built arcade modeled on the Rue de Rivoli), the Spianada (one of the largest squares in Greece), and dense networks of stone alleys that have barely changed since the 17th century. The island is large and green — olive groves blanket the south, Mt. Pantokrator dominates the north, and quiet mountain villages like Old Perithia survive at the top. Food has Venetian-Italian-British layers (the British left a cricket club still active in the Spianada). Evenings can be quiet in village tavernas or lively in the Old Town's bars and small clubs. The overall mood is sophisticated-but-relaxed.

Kefalonia is simpler and wilder. Argostoli, the capital, was destroyed in the catastrophic 1953 earthquake and rebuilt — pleasant but not historic. Fiskardo (north) is the exception: it survived the earthquake, retains its Venetian architecture, and feels more like a polished harbour town. The interior is dominated by Mount Ainos (Greece's only national park on an island), with pine-fir forests and wild horses. Most visitors stay in Lassi or Argostoli (south), Skala (southeast), or Fiskardo (north) and accept that distances mean driving 30-60 minutes between sights. Evenings are tavernas and early nights; Skala has the closest thing to a nightlife scene. The crowd skews active and outdoorsy.

Logistics and cost

Both islands have airports with direct European flights — Corfu has more (London, Manchester, Frankfurt, Vienna, Rome, Milan, and others on direct routes; Kefalonia has fewer but covers the same major hubs in summer). Ferries from the mainland: Corfu from Igoumenitsa (1 hour), Kefalonia from Kyllini (1.5 hours). Corfu also has international ferries from Italy (Brindisi, Bari, Venice), making it accessible without flying.

Corfu is the more expensive island. Mid-range hotels in high season run €120-220 a night on Corfu; €90-180 on Kefalonia. Restaurants run €30-50 per person on Corfu (€60+ at the Old Town's top spots); Kefalonia €25-45. Car rental is similar (€35-55/day in season). For a week's trip with mid-range accommodation, expect Corfu to cost €400-800 more than Kefalonia. Note that Kefalonia genuinely requires a car (the island scores 5/5 on "car needed"); Corfu can be done with limited driving if you base in the Old Town and use buses.

How long should you stay?

Corfu rewards 5-7 days because of the variety. Two days for the Old Town and Achilleion Palace. Two days for north-east coast beaches (Kassiopi, Paleokastritsa, Sidari). One day for a mountain village drive or Mt. Pantokrator. One day for a southern beach or an Olive Museum visit. A week feels genuinely full without rushing.

Kefalonia ideally wants 5-7 days too, because the island is huge (over an hour from north to south) and the highlights are spread out. A serious Kefalonia trip: Myrtos and the west coast, Fiskardo, Argostoli and its turtles, the Melissani-Drogarati cave pair, Skala and the southeast beaches, and at least one drive into Ainos. Going for less than 5 days means making hard cuts.

The honest verdict

This is a closer call than most Greek-island comparisons. The two islands score within 0.1 of each other overall, and both are legitimate choices for similar travelers. The real differentiator is what you want from a Greek-island trip. If you want the trip to be substantially about beaches and dramatic landscapes, with culture as the backdrop, choose Kefalonia. If you want the trip to be about culture, food, and town atmosphere with beaches as a supporting role, choose Corfu. For first-time visitors to Greece who care equally about both, Corfu is the marginally safer pick — it's easier without a car, has a stronger central anchor in the Old Town, and is harder to disappoint with. For active travelers, couples in their 30s-50s, and anyone willing to drive: Kefalonia delivers a more visually memorable trip. The Captain Corelli's Mandolin question — whether nostalgia for the book or film should influence the choice — is a tie-breaker; if it matters to you, Kefalonia.

Common questions

Which is better for first-time visitors to Greece, Corfu or Kefalonia?

Corfu, marginally. Its UNESCO Old Town gives a strong cultural anchor that newcomers often appreciate, restaurants and infrastructure are more developed, and you can manage without a car if you base in the Old Town. Kefalonia is a more dramatic island visually but really requires a road-trip mindset to be enjoyed properly — better for repeat visitors to Greece or active travelers.

Is Kefalonia or Corfu more expensive?

Corfu, by 20-30%. Mid-range hotels in high season run €120-220 on Corfu vs €90-180 on Kefalonia. Restaurants run €30-50 per person on Corfu (€60+ at top Old Town spots); Kefalonia €25-45. The premium reflects Corfu's stronger international flight access and more developed restaurant scene. For a week with mid-range accommodation, expect Corfu to cost €400-800 more than Kefalonia.

Do I need a car in Corfu or Kefalonia?

On Kefalonia, yes — the island is huge (773 km²) and the highlights are spread out; the car-need score is 5/5. On Corfu, you can manage without one if you base in the Old Town and use buses for nearby beaches, though a car opens up the rest of the island significantly. The car-need score for Corfu is 4/5. Realistically: rent a car on Kefalonia for the whole trip, rent on Corfu for 2-3 days within a 5-7 day stay.

Which has Myrtos Beach?

Kefalonia. Myrtos is on Kefalonia's northwest coast — a 1km crescent of white pebbles backed by 200-meter limestone cliffs. It's one of the most photographed beaches in Greece and a major reason people pick Kefalonia over Corfu. The descent down to it requires a winding drive, but the view from the overlook at the top is iconic on its own.

Can I do Corfu and Kefalonia in one trip?

Possible but inefficient. There's no direct fast ferry between them, and they sit at opposite ends of the Ionian chain. You'd either need to drive/ferry through the mainland (about 5 hours) or take two separate flights. For a 7-day trip, picking one and going deeper is better. If you really want both, plan 10-12 days and use Patras or Athens as a connection point — or fly between Corfu and Athens, then take the ferry from Kyllini to Kefalonia.
