Our verdict

The short answer: Chania for the better Crete entry point — its own airport, the iconic western beaches (Elafonisi, Balos, Falassarna) within driving range, and the most polished restaurant scene in Crete outside Heraklion. Rethymno for a quieter, more centrally-located base with arguably the more intimate Venetian old town and the Fortezza castle that dominates the skyline. The overall scores favor Chania (4.8 vs 3.8), but the gap reflects Chania's broader infrastructure rather than a "better town" — the choice is really about what you want from a Cretan base, and both towns deliver excellent versions of the Venetian-Ottoman-Greek north-coast experience.

Choose Chania if…

  • You want direct flights. Chania (CHQ) has an international airport with summer service from across Europe and year-round Athens flights. Rethymno has no airport — you'd use Chania or Heraklion (both about 1 hour by car).
  • You want the famous western Crete beach trips. Elafonisi (pink sand), Balos (lagoon), Falassarna (long sand with sunsets), and Seitan Limania are all 60-90 minutes from Chania. From Rethymno they're 90 minutes to 2.5 hours away.
  • You want a broader restaurant and bar scene. Chania town has roughly 4-5x more restaurant variety than Rethymno, including international cuisines, refined fusion, and several genuinely high-end places (Tamam, Salis, Wellness Tetris).
  • You're going for 5-7 days specifically in western Crete. Chania anchors that geography better.
  • You value the most dramatic Venetian harbor scenes. Chania's harbor with the lighthouse and the mosque (Mosque of the Janissaries) is among the most photographed in the Mediterranean.

Choose Rethymno if…

  • You want a quieter base. Rethymno gets meaningfully fewer visitors than Chania and the old town stays more relaxed even in peak August.
  • You're planning to drive both east and west across Crete. Rethymno is the geographic midpoint of the north coast — equidistant from Knossos (Heraklion area) and the western beaches.
  • You want a more intimate old town. Rethymno's Venetian old town is denser, more compact, and feels older despite both towns having similar Venetian foundations.
  • You want to climb the Fortezza. Rethymno's Fortezza is the largest preserved Venetian fortress in Greece — walking the walls at sunset is one of the better Cretan experiences and doesn't exist on Chania.
  • You like university-town energy. The University of Crete is based in Rethymno, giving the town a younger atmosphere outside peak tourist season.

Beaches: the biggest gap, and it's mostly about access

The 5.0 vs 3.8 beach score is the largest real difference. Both towns have beaches; the surrounding region is where they diverge.

Chania's beach proposition is the western Crete coastline. Elafonisi (90 minutes southwest) has pink-tinted sand and shallow turquoise lagoons — one of the most photographed beaches in Europe. Balos (1.5-2.5 hours northwest, by 4WD road or ferry from Kissamos) is a remote lagoon between two peninsulas, frequently called the most beautiful beach in Greece. Falassarna (1.5 hours west) has long sand and dramatic sunsets. Seitan Limania (45 minutes from Chania) is a tiny dramatic cove like a Norwegian fjord. None are within walking distance, but with a rental car they're an accessible day trip from Chania.

Rethymno's local beach is a long sand strip directly east of the old town — 12 km of organized sun-beds and tavernas, family-friendly and easy. For more dramatic options, the south coast is the move — Plakias (1 hour south) has good sand and clear water, Preveli (75 minutes south) has the famous palm-tree river-meets-sea scenery, Triopetra (90 minutes south) is wilder and quieter. These compete with Chania's western beaches on quality but not on iconic-photo status. The Chania western trio (Elafonisi, Balos, Falassarna) genuinely doesn't have a Rethymno-side equivalent.

One honest note: the famous Chania beaches are *busy* in peak July-August. Elafonisi can have 5,000+ visitors on a peak day; Balos boat tours run packed in summer. The "best beach in Greece" experience isn't always quiet. Rethymno's south-coast alternatives are meaningfully less crowded.

The old towns: different versions of the same heritage

Both towns share Venetian foundations (Crete was Venetian from 1204 to 1669) followed by Ottoman rule (1669-1898). Both old towns reflect this layered history. They feel surprisingly distinct in practice.

Chania's old town is larger, more layered, and more international in feel. The Venetian harbor curves around the lighthouse (rebuilt by the Egyptians in the 1830s but on Venetian foundations). The Mosque of the Janissaries (a 1645 Ottoman mosque, now an exhibition space) sits at the harbor's edge. The old town is divided into the Topanas quarter (waterfront, restaurants), the Splantzia quarter (inland, more residential and authentic), and the Jewish quarter (Evraiki) with the restored Etz Hayyim synagogue. Distances are walkable but the old town spreads over a meaningful area.

Rethymno's old town is smaller and feels more concentrated. The Venetian harbor is more intimate — fishing boats, restaurants on the curve, the lighthouse at the end. The Fortezza castle, built 1573-1580 to defend against Ottoman attacks, dominates the skyline and walking its walls at sunset offers some of the best urban-Cretan views available. The streets inside the old town are narrow and densely packed — you stumble onto small squares, hidden churches, Venetian mansions, Ottoman fountains. The Rimondi Fountain (built 1626) and the Neratzes Mosque are landmarks. The whole old town fits in 600 × 400 meters; you can cross it on foot in 10 minutes and explore it in detail in a day.

Food: Chania broader, Rethymno more local

Both towns offer excellent Cretan cuisine — dakos, kalitsounia, antikristo lamb, sfakian pie, plus seafood — but the scene is shaped differently.

Chania has the broader and more polished restaurant landscape. Tamam in the old Turkish baths is the canonical Cretan-meets-Levantine fusion. Salis on the waterfront is the formal-quality option. Wellness Tetris and Almyvita are newer additions to a serious dining scene. The Splantzia quarter has smaller authentic tavernas (Akrogiali, Apostolis). International cuisines (Italian, Asian, French) are also represented — uncommon for Cretan towns of this size. Restaurant density is high enough that you can eat genuinely well at several price points across a week without repeating.

Rethymno's food scene is smaller but arguably more locally distinctive. Lemonokipos and Avli are the standouts — both serving refined Cretan cuisine in restored Venetian mansions with garden settings. Castelvecchio is excellent for traditional. The smaller authentic tavernas (Stou Anifanti, Sou Magkas) are easier to find than in Chania's tourist-heavier old town. Prices run 20-30% lower than Chania equivalents. The scene feels more like "the best of local Cretan food" than "Cretan food plus international options."

The geographic position matters

Chania is in western Crete. Driving distances from Chania: 60 km to Rethymno (1 hour), 140 km to Heraklion (1.5-2 hours), 90 km to Elafonisi (90 minutes), 70 km to the Samaria Gorge bus departure (1 hour). For someone basing in Chania and exploring east, even Heraklion becomes a substantial day trip.

Rethymno sits at the geographic middle of the north coast. From Rethymno: 60 km west to Chania (1 hour), 80 km east to Heraklion (1 hour), 90 km southwest to Plakias (1 hour), 110 km east to Knossos (1.5 hours), 90 km west to Elafonisi (1.5 hours). Day trips in both directions are practical. For travelers who want to do "all of Crete" rather than focus on the west, Rethymno is the better base.

This is the strongest argument for Rethymno as a base — the practical access to Crete as a whole. The strongest argument for Chania is the depth of what's directly nearby (the dramatic western beaches, the Akrotiri monasteries, the White Mountains).

How long should you stay?

Chania works at 4-7 days. Two days for Chania town and the harbor. One day for Elafonisi (full day). One day for Balos (full day, via Kissamos boat or 4WD road). One day for the Samaria Gorge if you're a hiker (16km descent, 5-6 hours, requires the 6 AM bus). One day for Falassarna or the Akrotiri peninsula. A week is fully booked without rushing.

Rethymno works at 3-5 days as a standalone destination, or 4-7 days as a base for exploring Crete more broadly. Two days for Rethymno town and the Fortezza. One day for the south coast (Plakias, Preveli, Triopetra). One day for Knossos or Heraklion. Optional day for the Arkadi Monastery (the national-symbol-of-resistance site, 25 km southeast). For travelers using Rethymno as a Crete-wide base, additional days for further excursions.

The honest verdict

The original short-form thesis remains right: choose Chania for the best of western Crete (and the airport advantage), choose Rethymno for a quieter base with the better Fortezza experience and central Crete positioning. But after spending more time with both towns, a few refinements: Chania's reputation as "better" is largely about beach proximity, not about the town itself. Rethymno's old town is arguably more beautifully preserved and feels more like a working Greek town rather than a tourism-anchor. For first-time visitors to Crete who want the iconic beach photos: Chania. For repeat Crete visitors or travelers who want to see Crete broadly: Rethymno. For travelers wanting maximum beach quality with willingness to drive: Chania, no contest. For travelers wanting a more relaxed pace with equal cultural depth: Rethymno is slightly better. The combination works very well — 3-4 days Chania, 2-3 days Rethymno, with a stop at Knossos en route to or from Heraklion airport. This is genuinely the natural Crete-week-itinerary structure for travelers who want to see meaningful portions of the island.

Common questions

Which has the better old town, Chania or Rethymno?

Both old towns are excellent, with the same Venetian-Ottoman heritage. Chania's is larger and more layered (the Topanas, Splantzia, and Evraiki quarters each have distinct character), with the iconic harbor scene with the lighthouse and the Mosque of the Janissaries. Rethymno's is smaller, more concentrated, and arguably more intimate — the Venetian harbor is more compact, the Fortezza castle dominates the skyline (Chania has no equivalent), and the streets are denser. For a more spectacular waterfront: Chania. For a more preserved, walkable old town: Rethymno. Personal preference is the deciding factor here.

Should I base in Chania or Rethymno for a Crete trip?

It depends on what you want to see. For a western-Crete-focused trip with the famous beaches (Elafonisi, Balos, Falassarna) and the Samaria Gorge: Chania, definitively. For a Crete-wide trip that includes Knossos, the south coast, and excursions in multiple directions: Rethymno, because of its central position. For a 7+ day trip seeing meaningful portions of Crete, the combination of 3-4 nights Chania + 2-3 nights Rethymno is the natural arrangement — see both, base where you're driving from, and avoid one base for the whole trip.

Is Rethymno worth visiting if I'm based in Chania?

Yes — Rethymno is 1 hour from Chania by car (75 km on the E75 north coast highway) and works well as a day trip. A typical day: drive in the morning, walk the Fortezza walls (2-3 hours), lunch at one of the old town tavernas, wander the streets, drive back. The Arkadi Monastery (25 km east of Rethymno) is often added to the trip — the national monument of Cretan resistance against the Ottomans. From Chania, you can do Rethymno + Arkadi as a full day excursion.

What about the Samaria Gorge — is it accessible from Rethymno?

Less practically than from Chania. The Samaria Gorge bus departs from Chania at 6 AM, with the hike taking 5-6 hours (16 km descent). From Rethymno you'd need to drive 1 hour to Chania first, but the 6 AM bus departure makes this an awkward early start. Alternative: Rethymno-based travelers often do the Imbros Gorge instead — it's south of Rethymno, takes 2-3 hours to hike (much easier), and has similar scenic value at a fraction of the effort. The Samaria Gorge has more dramatic walls, but Imbros is the practical Rethymno-base hike.

Does Rethymno have a beach?

Yes — the Rethymno town beach is a 12 km strip of organized sand directly east of the old town. It's family-friendly, with shallow water and a long line of beach bars and tavernas along the coastal road. It's not iconic in the way Elafonisi or Balos are, but it's perfectly usable for daily swimming and is meaningfully closer than the famous Chania beaches (which require 60-90 minute drives from Chania town). For more dramatic Rethymno-area beaches, the south coast (Plakias, Preveli, Triopetra) offers excellent options 1 hour south.
